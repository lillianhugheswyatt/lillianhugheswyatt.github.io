<h2 style="font-size: 30px;">
        Controlled creation of quantum defects in diamond
  </h2>

  <img
    class="thrust-figure-left"  
    src="{{ '/assets/img/Thrust1_website.png' | relative_url }}"
    alt="Diamond spin systems">
      
  <p>
    We aim to create spin defects with controlled configurations and tailored quantum properties via diamond growth, enabling new capabilities for quantum sensing, communication, and simulation. 
    </p>
    <p>
      Vacancy-related centers in diamond involving group IV elements, e.g., silicon (SiV), germanium (GeV), tin (SnV), and lead (PbV), have emerged as promising spin–photon interfaces due to their split-vacancy structure (see Figure) which yields reduced sensitivity to electric field noise, narrow zero-phonon lines with strong emission, and efficient, coherent optical access to spin degrees of freedom.
      However, formation of group IV centers typically relies on the stochastic and uncontrolled process of ion implantation, which causes lattice damage including paramagnetic defects, charge traps, and strain, and ultimately results in degraded optical and spin properties.
       incorporation of group IV color centers via doping during CVD will overcome the limits of implantation and enable unparalleled control over defect properties and configurations, as similarly demonstrated in our previous work with nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers.
      We will use a variety of synthesis-based tuning knobs to achieve successful doping of group IV color centers with precise depth confinement while also improving creation efficiency and charge state stability.
    </p>
  </div>

<div class="thrust-section">
  
  <h2 style="font-size: 30px;">
    Atomic-scale processing of surfaces and superconducting heterostructures
  </h2>

  <img
    class="thrust-figure-right" 
    src="{{ '/assets/img/Thrust3_website.png' | relative_url }}"
    alt="Atomic layer processing">
      
  <p>
       We will realize atomic-scale control of surfaces and thin films with atomic layer processing to understand and mitigate sources of loss that limit quantum defects and superconducting heterostructures. 
    </p>
    <p>
      We will use atomic layer etching (ALE) and atomic layer deposition (ALD) to control and refine surfaces of both diamond and superconducting thin films.
      The majority of superconducting qubit technologies rely on aluminum-based junctions, whose properties are constrained by uncontrolled amorphous oxide tunneling barriers and interfaces that suffer from defects, disorder, and atomic-scale variations.
      Atomic layer processing offers a route to overcome these limitations while enabling complex, multilayer architectures through conformal growth and precise interface control.
      Our goal is to extend these capabilities to high-quality, epitaxial ultra-thin films, combining crystalline quality with the versatility and scalability of atomic layer processing.
    </p>
  </div>


<!-- ========== RESEARCH THRUST 3 ========== -->
<div class="thrust-section">

<h2 style="font-size: 30px;"> Monolithic hybrid quantum systems in diamond</h2>

  <img
      class="thrust-figure-left"  
      src="{{ '/assets/img/Thrust2_website.png' | relative_url }}"
      alt="Hybrid quantum systems">

  <p>
      We aim to create a unified materials platform for coherent spin–superconductor coupling and opportunities for novel hybrid quantum systems by achieving spatially-defined superconductivity in diamond through precision boron doping.
    </p>
  <p>
      Coupling superconducting circuits with solid-state spin defects could enable long-lived quantum memories and coherent microwave-to-optical interfaces, addressing critical challenges in quantum information storage and long-distance communication. 
    We hypothesize that boron-doping-induced superconductivity in diamond can be harnessed to embed a superconducting circuit very close to a spin defect and achieve strong, coherent coupling while also eliminating lossy interfaces associated with dissimilar materials stacks.
    By spatially defining superconducting and insulating regions through boron doping during CVD, our bottom-up approach will replace heterogeneous interfaces with doping-defined electronic transitions.
    We anticipate that this monolithic integration platform will unlock numerous exciting possibilities for scalable and coherent hybrid quantum technologies.
  </p>
